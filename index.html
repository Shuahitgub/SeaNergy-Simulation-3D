What happens in each cell

Zinc is more active than copper in seawater, so it gives up electrons: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. The electrons travel through the wires, the switch and the LED to the copper plate, where dissolved oxygen takes them: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. Inside the seawater, ions carry the current back between the plates, which closes the circuit.

Why 8 cells in series

One zinc–copper cell gives well under 1 V, but the 3–4 V LED needs a minimum voltage before it lights. The yellow wires join the copper (+) of each cell to the zinc (−) of the next, so the voltages add: V = n × E. With 8 cells at about 0.7 V each that is roughly 5.6 V before any load. The same current flows through every cell.

Why each cell needs its own compartment

The model splits the round tank into 8 sealed wedge compartments, one per Zn–Cu pair. Your sketch shows one shared tank, so this is a change. If all 8 pairs sat in one shared pool of seawater, the water would connect every plate to every other plate. Current would leak through the seawater from cell to cell instead of going through the LED, and the total voltage would fall toward that of a single cell. Series cells only add up when their electrolytes are kept apart.

Where the light goes

The LED sits at the bottom of the 126×48.5×33.5 mm aluminium reflector cup, which gathers its light into a narrow upward beam (the 15–20° on your sketch is that beam angle). The beam hits the honeycomb reflective tape ring on the underside of the lid and comes back down into the clear tapered shade. Retro-reflective tape sends most light back toward its source, so a white or mirror ring would spread the light wider. Either way, the bounce off the lid is what makes the shade glow instead of only lighting the ceiling.

The model behind the numbers

  • Each cell: voltage E with internal resistance r = relectrolyte + relectrode.
  • relectrolyte = gap ÷ (σ × A), with a 0.6 cm plate gap and A = immersed area of one plate face (2.6 cm wide).
  • relectrode = (electrode loss) ÷ (wetted area). This lumps the reaction losses at both plates; calibrate it from a measurement.
  • LED: off below its turn-on voltage, then V = Von + I × RLED.
  • Current: I = (nE − Von) ÷ (n·r + RLED), or zero if that is negative or the switch is off.
  • Zinc used per anode: m = I·t·M ÷ (2F), with M = 65.38 g/mol and F = 96 485 C/mol.

Default values and where they come from

  • Cell voltage 0.70 V: zinc sits at about −0.98 to −1.03 V and copper at about −0.30 to −0.57 V in a seawater galvanic series, so a Zn–Cu pair gives roughly 0.4–0.7 V open circuit. Galvanic series table
  • Conductivity 5.0 S/m: standard seawater (salinity 35) is 4.29 S/m at 15 °C, and conductivity rises with temperature. RBR: practical salinity of seawater
  • Plates: 2.6 cm wide × 8.2 cm tall, 0.1 cm thick zinc (assumed), 0.6 cm gap. Electrode loss 300 Ω·cm² and the LED values are illustrative starting points, not SeaNergy measurements.

Important: this is a teaching simulation. Replace the defaults with your measured cell voltage and current before quoting any number from it.
